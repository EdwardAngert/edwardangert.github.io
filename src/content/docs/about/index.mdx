My first computer was a collection of heavy boxes that we needed a flat cart to bring to the car.

A few years later, the morning after we bought a CD-ROM drive at a computer show, I woke up early and started disassembling my computer.
It was the mid 90s, and I was in fifth grade.

By the time my parents woke up, I was mesmerized by the music and sounds in the game Myst, unsuccessfully trying to figure out how to play a piano inside the game.

A year or two later, I was calling Red Hat, asking them to "help me uninstall Linux."

A year or two after that, we had some form of internet at home.

Another year or two, and I learned HTML out of a book.

Since then, it seems like every few years brings a new unexpected leap in technology that quickly becomes our everyday.

From that first computer to the phones in our pockets, the Pi Zero on my desk, and smart glasses, we've come a long way in how we use technology.

There's an idea that's come up with other parents of young children that I think applies to technology too:

> The day is coming when our children will have to explain to us something we can't even yet imagine.

It's scary, and exciting, and I can't wait.

## Hello, this is Edward

An accidental catchphrase from the days of crowding around a conference table, leaning toward the microphone, and announcing myself for the conference call.

At the time, I was the office assistant at a small non-profit, and that meant that I answered phones, set up the space, and ordered supplies.

Being a small non-profit, it also meant I:

<ColumnList columns={2}>

- Handled vendor relationships
- Dealt with business insurance
- Was the health insurance plans administrator
- Maintained the employee handbook
- Implemented remote access to the in-house server (and trained the staff on it)
- Replaced the telephone service with VoIP (and trained the staff on it)
- Led bike rides
- Bought snacks
- Washed dishes

</ColumnList>

I also developed a knowledge system that used tools the staff was already familiar with and taught them how to use it so that everyone owned content and knew how to find it.

We didn't call it documentation or business process optimization, but effectively, that's what I was doing with my degree in professional communications and technical writing before I had the Technical Writer title.

Growing up, I never had an answer for "what do I want to be when I grow up."
And that's a good thing - because I had no idea what this future would look like; that I could make a career out of learning something inside out and having people share their expertise with me, and combining it with my love for teaching so that someone else could use that knowledge themselves.

Growing up, my toys were always in various stages of disassembly.
As an adult, I'll rearrange the kitchen drawers to put things we use most often in easier to reach places, and I've rehung doors and moved light switches because their flow felt awkward.

That's the gist then, I guess.
There's always room for improvement, and I'm always eager to find it.

## How I work

I want to hear every little detail that you're excited about.

I want the long version of the story.
And I want the version you haven't rehearsed, the one with the details you forgot or didn't think people care about.
I do, and I want to know them.

That's a gift in technical writing too, a talent for breaking the story and finding the vignette that was hiding on the side, influenced by some other detail.

This too is documentation.

Often, a team's knowledge just hasn't been excavated and written down.
Equally often, that doesn't become a problem until a new team member is onboarded, or until one is writing a transition doc on their way out.

I work to make that easier, less stressful, and more findable.
Using whatever tool is least disruptive.

### Better, together

A long while back, a candidate I was interviewing told me about the printouts he hung on the outside of his cubicle with grammatical errors he found in other people's work.

It reminded me of bullies making fun of a kid's or their parent's accent.
I pore over details in grammar and try to follow the rules when they make sense to.
Interviews are hard and unfair, and I'm open to forgiving a lot of missteps, but my teams and I value good information more than well-placed apostrophes, and we work hard to establish trust with SMEs who feel comfortable sharing with us.

I will cut and reorganize your draft ruthlessly, and I trust you to do the same for mine.

But just the words.
Don't be a bully.

### Wherever you are

If we've worked together, you've probably seen it in the Zoom chat as soon as the meeting starts:

> FYI: Can't tell your KPIs from your P&L?
>
> Here's a link to the glossary of acronyms and initialisms:
> IYKYK.

Documentation is everywhere, and it should be broadly defined.

1. It's only effective if it exists.
1. It's only effective if you can find it.

Write it down and make sure people can find it.

Technical writing is the process, systems, and philosophy around how to improve all of that, but it all starts with you and making sure you know that you have information worth sharing.

I just help get it out of you in ways that help your audience.
